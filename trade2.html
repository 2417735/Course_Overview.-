Labor Productivity & Comparative Advantage

1. Comprehensive Reading Notes

The Ricardian architecture isolates labor as the sole input variable to prove that wage variations reflect underlying national productivity trends. When open trade structures emerge, price convergence forces industries to reallocate resources toward activities with the lowest relative opportunity costs.

2. Guiding Questions & Core Answers

Q1: What is the fundamental difference between "absolute advantage" and "comparative advantage," and which one determines trade?

Absolute Advantage measures absolute raw efficiency—a country requires fewer labor hours to output a unit of a good than its trading partner. Comparative Advantage measures relative efficiency—a country's opportunity cost of producing a good is lower than its partner's. **Comparative advantage alone determines international trade patterns.**

Q2: How do we prove that a low-productivity country can still compete and benefit from trade with a high-productivity "superpower"?

Even if a superpower is absolutely more efficient at manufacturing everything, it cannot possess a comparative advantage in everything. The superpower faces high opportunity costs to produce lower-value goods. By specializing in its areas of greatest relative advantage and outsourcing others, it creates space for the low-productivity country to successfully export goods where its absolute disadvantage is smallest. This integration maximizes real purchasing power for consumers in both nations.

Q3: Interpret the general shape of the PPF model and draw a little picture for me.

In a standard multi-factor economy, the Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) is **concave (bowed-out from the origin)**. This shape reflects increasing opportunity costs: as production expands in one sector, the economy must pull resources that are progressively less suited to it from other sectors, requiring greater sacrifices of alternative goods per unit produced.
