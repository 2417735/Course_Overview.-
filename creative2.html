Systems Thinking & Economies of Scale

1. Defining a System

A system is defined as a group of interconnected components forming a unified whole. It is characterized by structures where inputs are transformed through a process into outputs, regulating the internal stocks and flows of resources.

Deconstructing System Paradigms (The Tree Model)

Systems thinking requires prioritizing dynamic functional loops over static anatomy:

  • Anatomical Fallacy Roots → Trunk → Branches → Leaves → Flower → Fruit

    This list maps physical parts rather than structural system dynamics.

  • Functional System Leaves (Light Input) → Photosynthesis (Process) → Energy Allocation to All Parts & Oxygen (Outputs)

    This framework correctly maps resource transfers across system lines.

2. Methodology of Systems Thinking

To analyze an economic structure through this framework, we follow five steps to map out how separate components interact:

  • Define Boundaries: Isolating the internal process from external noise.
  • Identify the Parts: Cataloging all physical assets, information inputs, and labor inputs.
  • Identify Relationships: Mapping out how changing one asset changes the productivity of others.
  • Identify Flows & Stocks: Measuring how quickly materials move through storage pools.
  • Identify the Function: Uncovering the core operational goal of the system loop.

3. Application: The Simple Factory Loop

A factory operates as an engineering loop that uses capital investments to accelerate resource throughput:

Materials → Equipment → Workers → IP → Product Output → Revenue / Profit → Raw Materials Reinjection

Mathematical Modeling of Internal Scale

By adjusting input ratios within fixed cost boundaries, the system increases output velocity, compressing per-unit asset costs:

Baseline (1 Machine) 5 Workers 100 Units $1,000 $10.00
Scaled System (5 Machines) 5 Workers 500 Units $1,000 $2.00 (Cost Reduction)

4. Balancing Loops: Systemic Constraints to Scaling

This optimization path cannot continue indefinitely. Every system eventually hits boundaries imposed by external balancing loops, which stall unchecked exponential growth:

  • Demand Saturation: Shifting output beyond what the market can absorb gluts storage stocks and tanks unit prices.
  • Competitive Reaction: Emerging competitors enter the ecosystem to capture excess yields, diluting localized market share.
  • Regulatory Boundaries: Legal constraints, environmental rules, or zoning laws alter the system's cost structure.
  • Technological Obsolescence: External disruptive inventions render the entire production loop obsolete, breaking the reinjection cycle.
