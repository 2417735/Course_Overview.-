Reading Assignment 3 • Textbook Chapter 5

Resources, Comparative Advantage, & Income Distribution

1. Comprehensive Reading Notes

The Heckscher-Ohlin framework expands on Ricardo's model by introducing multiple factors of production: **Capital (K)** and **Labor (L)**. It illustrates how relative resource scarcity across borders shapes global trade patterns and alters the internal distribution of national income.

2. Guiding Questions & Core Answers

Q1: How does the Heckscher-Ohlin model differ from the Ricardian model in terms of construction and predictive potential?

The Ricardian model assumes only **one factor of production (labor)** and attributes comparative advantage entirely to cross-border **technology and productivity differences**. The Heckscher-Ohlin model assumes **multiple factors (capital, labor, land)**, treats technologies as identical across countries, and attributes comparative advantage to differences in **national factor endowments**. This allows the Heckscher-Ohlin model to predict internal conflicts over income distribution among resource owners.

Q2: Explain factor price equalization and why specific factors might not equalize in reality?

The **Factor Price Equalization Theorem** states that international trade leads to the complete convergence of real returns to identical production factors (wages for labor, rental rates for capital) across trading nations. By trading goods, countries indirectly trade factor services, substituting for the physical migration of labor and capital.

Frictional Realities Preventing Equalization:

  • Pervasive Trade Barriers: Tariffs, quotas, and transport costs prevent international goods prices from equalizing completely.
  • Technological Asymmetries: Contrary to the model's assumptions, countries do not use identical production technologies in practice.
  • Specialization Boundaries: If factor endowment differences are extreme, countries may specialize completely in different goods, breaking the mathematical link required for price equalization.
