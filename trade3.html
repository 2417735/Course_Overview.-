Reading Assignment 3 • Textbook Chapter 5

Resources, Comparative Advantage, & Income Distribution

1. Comprehensive Reading Notes

Based on Chapter 5 of Paul Krugman's International Trade: Theory and Policy, the Heckscher-Ohlin framework expands significantly on simpler trade paradigms by modeling multiple factors of production: **Capital ($K$)** and **Labor ($L$)**[cite: 2]. It uncovers how relative resource scarcity across political borders structurally shapes global trade vectors and alters the internal distribution of national wealth[cite: 2].

Heckscher-Ohlin Model vs. Ricardian Model

The Ricardian Model: Built as a one-factor economy where labor ($L$) is the sole input[cite: 2]. Comparative advantage is driven entirely by international differences in labor productivity (technological differences), resulting in a straight-line PPF with a constant opportunity cost[cite: 2].

The Heckscher-Ohlin Model: Built as a multi-factor economy (typically a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ framework: two countries, two goods, and two mobile factors—Capital $K$ and Labor $L$)[cite: 2]. Comparative advantage is driven by the interaction between a nation's resource endowments (factor abundance) and the industry's production technology (factor intensity)[cite: 2].

Because inputs cannot perfectly substitute for one another across distinct manufacturing sectors, the Heckscher-Ohlin model features a curved production frontier displaying an increasing opportunity cost profile[cite: 2].

2. Guiding Questions & Core Answers

Q1 How do the predictive potentials of these models diverge regarding trade patterns and income distribution?

Trade Patterns: The Ricardian model predicts that a country will export goods where it has an absolute productivity edge[cite: 2]. The Heckscher-Ohlin model predicts that a country will export goods that intensively use its abundant factor and import goods that use its scarce factor[cite: 2].

Income Distribution: In the Ricardian framework, trade benefits every worker because labor is mobile and homogeneous[cite: 2]. Conversely, the Heckscher-Ohlin model predicts long-run domestic conflict: trade systematically benefits the owners of a country's abundant factor but hurts the owners of its scarce factor[cite: 2].
Q2 What is Factor-Price Equalization?
The theorem posits that international trade in goods acts as an implicit substitute for the physical migration of factors[cite: 2]. When a labor-abundant nation exports labor-intensive products, it is effectively exporting its abundant labor embedded in the product[cite: 2]. In an idealized, friction-free market, free trade will completely equalize the absolute wages of homogeneous labor and the rental rates of capital across nations[cite: 2].
Q3 Why do absolute factor prices fail to equalize in real-world contexts?
Absolute factor prices diverge drastically between countries due to several severe market frictions[cite: 2]:
  • Barriers to Trade: Natural barriers like transportation costs and restrictions like tariffs or quotas prevent goods prices from fully equalizing, blocking factor convergence[cite: 2].
  • Technological Asymmetries: In practice, a worker backed by advanced infrastructure and superior processes will be drastically more productive—and earn a higher real wage—than an identical worker using outdated methods[cite: 2].
  • Specialization Dynamics: Equalization requires that both countries continue to produce both goods[cite: 2]. If a trade shock causes a country to completely specialize in only one good, factor prices are no longer bound to equalize[cite: 2].

Core Takeaway: Domestic Income Conflict

Unlike alternative trade frameworks, Heckscher-Ohlin proves that trade liberalization creates concentrated domestic winners and losers—systematically decreasing the real income of scarce resource providers while boosting abundant capital owners[cite: 2].
